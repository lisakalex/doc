Capacity Isn't a Setting

One line of your Terraform decides how the table is billed. Nothing in your Terraform decides how much throughput it can actually deliver — that comes from the keys, one partition at a time, and the ceiling is identical in both billing modes.

terraform/main.tf:114 PAY_PER_REQUEST 3,000 read / 1,000 write units per partition no maximum set

Start with the line you already wrote

The billing mode is the easy half, and yours is right

# terraform/main.tf:112 resource "aws_dynamodb_table" "users" { name = local.dynamodb_table billing_mode = "PAY_PER_REQUEST" hash_key = "email" }

PAY_PER_REQUEST is on-demand mode: you are billed per request unit consumed, and nothing at all for an idle table. The alternative is PROVISIONED, where you declare a number of capacity units per second and pay for that number every hour of the month whether you consume it or not. The Terraform provider's default is PROVISIONED; this table overrides it.

Since 1 November 2024 that override is the right one for most tables, and AWS says so in its own words: "most provisioned capacity workloads on DynamoDB today will achieve a lower price with on-demand mode." On-demand is now the documented default and the documented recommendation.

So we can stop looking at it

The billing line in this repo is correct. Two other things about this table's throughput are not, and neither of them is a capacity setting.

The economics, exactly

The whole provisioned-versus-on-demand argument is one ratio

List price, us-east-1, standard table class:

On-demandProvisioned
Writes$0.625 per million write request units$0.00065 per WCU-hour
Reads$0.125 per million read request units$0.00013 per RCU-hour
Storage$0.25 per GB-month$0.25 per GB-month

One capacity unit held for one hour buys 3,600 request units of work. That is the only conversion needed to compare them:

write 3,600 × $0.625/1,000,000 = $0.00225 on-demand $0.00065 provisioned --------- 3.46× read 3,600 × $0.125/1,000,000 = $0.00045 on-demand $0.00013 provisioned --------- 3.46×

The two ratios are the same to three figures — AWS priced all four numbers off one multiple. And eventual consistency halves the units consumed in both modes equally, so it cancels out and does not move the comparison. Which means the entire mode question collapses to one number:

The only number in it

On-demand costs 3.46× per unit of work. Provisioned is cheaper if you consume more than 1 ÷ 3.46 = 28.9% of what you provision, averaged over the month. Below that, on-demand wins. Everything else is detail.

Before 1 November 2024, on-demand writes were $1.25 per million — a ratio of 6.92× and a break-even of 14.4%. The price cut doubled the break-even utilisation overnight. Advice written before that date, in books or blog posts or your own notes, was computed against a number that is no longer true, and the error always points the same way: it recommends provisioned for workloads that are now cheaper on-demand.

Reserved capacity is the third option, and it only sharpens the same ratio: up to 54% off for a one-year term and 77% for three years, which moves the break-even to roughly 13% and 6.6%. The commitment is on a capacity number, so you are forecasting the shape of your traffic three years out, and an over-bought reservation is billed in full.

Why the default moved

Almost nobody runs at 29%

That is an ordinary web application, and it lands just above the line at 39% utilisation. This is why the price cut flipped the default: a normal diurnal curve sits near 29%, so halving the on-demand price moved most workloads of this shape across it.

Now read the second number in that figure. Provisioned wins by $159.23 a month, and that is the entire prize. To collect it you take on a provisioned number somebody has to choose, auto scaling to adjust it, alarms to tell you when the choice was wrong, and a throttling risk every morning at 09:00. On a $634 table that trade is bad. On a $63,400 table the same 25% is $15,900 a month and you hire someone to do nothing else.

The rule

Size the effort against the bill, not against the percentage. The mode decision is worth between 0% and 71% of your DynamoDB spend, and 71% only for traffic that never varies.

Auto scaling is not on-demand

The standard move here is to keep provisioned, switch on auto scaling and claim both. It does not work that way, and the documentation is unusually explicit about why. Target utilisation is settable between 20% and 90%, and the console offers 70%. Then:

StageWhat the docs say
metricconsumed capacity is published in one-minute periods
triggerauto scaling fires when consumption breaches the target for two consecutive minutes
alarmthe CloudWatch alarm "might have a short delay of up to a few minutes before triggering auto scaling"
applyUpdateTable "takes several minutes to update the provisioned capacity"
meanwhile"any requests that exceed the previous provisioned capacity of the tables are throttled"
scale downneeds 15 consecutive data points below target
decreases4 available at the start of each UTC day, +1 per hour, maximum 27 a day

So auto scaling follows a trend over several minutes. It cannot absorb a spike. What absorbs spikes is burst capacity: DynamoDB retains up to five minutes — 300 seconds — of your unused capacity and lets you spend it faster than your provisioned rate. That is real and it saves you often. It is also not a budget you can plan with: the same page says DynamoDB spends burst capacity "for background maintenance and other tasks without prior notice", and that "these burst capacity details might change in the future".

What a 70% target actually buys

Holding a 70% target means choosing to pay for 1.43× your consumption on the days everything works. Against on-demand's 3.46× that is still a 59% saving — on the days the target holds.

The limit with no mode

3,000 and 1,000, whichever mode you chose

From the partition key best-practices page, with no billing mode anywhere in the sentence: "Every partition in a DynamoDB table is designed to deliver a maximum capacity of 3,000 read units per second and 1,000 write units per second." On-demand does not raise it. Provisioning 40,000 WCU does not raise it. It is a property of a partition, and your partition key decides which items have to share one.

Item size counts twice here, because the limit is in units and not in operations. The docs' own example: a 20 KB item costs 5 read units per strongly consistent read, so a single such item serves 600 reads a second — not 3,000 — before its partition throttles.

Which means four different things throttle a DynamoDB request, and they are not interchangeable:

What was exceededModeExceptionCloudWatch metric
partition (key range) throughputbothThrottlingExceptionReadKeyRangeThroughputThrottleEvents

All three exceptions now carry a ThrottlingReason list, formatted ResourceType + OperationType + LimitType with the ARN of the affected resource — so the error itself tells you table or index, read or write, and which of the four limits: TableReadKeyRangeThroughputExceeded, TableWriteAccountLimitExceeded, IndexWriteMaxOnDemandThroughputExceeded. Read it before you change a capacity number. Three of the four are not fixed by capacity.

The metric that lies to you

ThrottledRequests increments once per request, no matter how many of the events inside it were throttled. A PutItem that writes the base table and three indexes counts as one throttle even if all four were refused. Alarm on the cause-specific metrics or you will systematically underestimate what is happening.

And a throttle is usually not an error you get to see. The SDKs retry both ThrottlingException and ProvisionedThroughputExceededException automatically, with exponential backoff. The first symptom of a hot partition is p99 latency, not a 400 — by the time your application logs an error, the retry queue has already given up.

The bill comes due in Lesson 5.1

You designed a hot partition last lesson

In 5.1 we invented signup_month so a date-range query would have something to aim at, and keyed a GSI on (signup_month, created_at). That index is the right answer to the read question and a textbook write anti-pattern. Both are true at once.

signup_month takes twelve values a year, and every new signup belongs to exactly one of them: the current month. So each put_user() writes two items — one to the base table, spread across its partitions by hash(email), and one to the index, on a single partition key, for thirty days at a stretch.

Adaptive capacity is not the escape hatch. It applies in both modes, it reacts by handing a hot partition more of the table's throughput, and if traffic concentrates on one key it will go as far as moving that single key onto a partition of its own — where, the docs say, DynamoDB "can deliver throughput up to the partition maximum of 3,000 RCUs and 1,000 WCUs to that single item's primary key". That is the end of the road. A key cannot be split. If the table has a local secondary index, adaptive capacity will not split item collections at all.

And the failure does not stay inside the index. As Lesson 5.1 noted, index write throttling pushes back on the base table, so what actually breaks is PutItem on users — a write to a table whose own key schema is perfect.

The honest accounting for this repo: 1,000 writes a second is 86 million signups a day, so the ceiling is nowhere near. It matters because the shape is the one that bites at scale, and you now own one. When it does matter the fix is write sharding — make the key signup_month#<0-9> with the suffix chosen at random, and the ceiling becomes 10,000 writes a second. You pay for it in reads: a month is now ten partition keys, so every query becomes ten queries, and the shard count is part of the contract with every reader from then on.

One level down, the same shape

created_at as the sort key means new items always land at the same end of the item collection. It is harmless at this size, and it is why the docs warn against sharding by hand on top of a sort key that increases monotonically.

The missing line

On-demand removes capacity planning, and the cost ceiling with it

The only limit on aws_dynamodb_table.users today is the account's default table-level quota: 40,000 request units a second.

40,000 write request units/s × 3,600 s = 144,000,000 writes 144,000,000 × $0.625 / 1,000,000 = $90.00 per hour $2,160 per day

A brand-new table cannot reach that immediately — warm throughput starts a fresh on-demand table at 4,000 writes and 12,000 reads a second, which caps a cold runaway at about $9 an hour. A table that has already served a real peak has no such brake.

Nothing in IAM prevents it either. dynamodb:PutItem is permission to write; there is no permission to spend. The protection is a table setting, and this repo does not set it:

# terraform/main.tf — the line that is missing resource "aws_dynamodb_table" "users" { name = local.dynamodb_table billing_mode = "PAY_PER_REQUEST" hash_key = "email" on_demand_throughput { max_read_request_units = 500 max_write_request_units = 100 } }

The same block nests inside global_secondary_index, so an index gets its own ceiling. The smallest value is 1 request unit per second, and -1 removes the limit.

Two caveats, both from the docs. The maximum "is applied on a best-effort basis and should be thought of as targets instead of guaranteed request ceilings", because burst capacity can carry you past it. And requests over the maximum get ThrottlingException, which your SDK retries silently — so the setting is a cost control, not a back-pressure signal, and it needs an alarm on WriteMaxOnDemandThroughputThrottleEvents to be visible to a human.

You have seen this failure already

August 2026 in this account: $34.96 of credit spent on an idle EKS control plane, because nothing capped it and nobody was watching. Same class of mistake. The capacity mode that removes the plan also removes the plan's upper bound, and the upper bound was the part that protected you.

What "elastic" means in practice

On-demand is elastic, not infinite

BehaviourThe actual number
Provisioned → on-demandup to four times in a rolling 24 hours
On-demand → provisionedany time
A brand-new on-demand tablesustains 4,000 writes/s and 12,000 reads/s immediately
Afterwardsabsorbs up to double the previous peak, instantly
Past double, inside 30 minutescan throttle; AWS advises spreading growth over at least 30 minutes, or pre-warming
Warm throughputvisible on every table, rises by itself with use, can be raised deliberately — and never decreases

Which makes the load test a trap

A cold table stepped from zero to 20,000 writes a second throttles. The same table ramped over half an hour does not. If your load test proves the table is ready, check whether it proved something about the ramp instead.

The method

Four steps, in this order

1

Get the ratio, not the opinion

ConsumedWriteCapacityUnits with the Sum statistic, divided by the period in seconds, gives consumed units per second. Mean ÷ peak over a representative month is your utilisation. Below 28.9%, stay on-demand and stop here. Above it, keep going — and compare the saving in dollars against the cost of managing it.

2

Divide the peak by the hottest key

Find the partition key taking the largest share of traffic; CloudWatch Contributor Insights names it for you. If that one key needs more than 1,000 write units or 3,000 read units a second, no billing mode helps and no capacity number helps. Shard the key, and do it before the data exists.

3

Give whichever mode you chose an upper bound

On-demand: on_demand_throughput. Provisioned: auto scaling's maximum, which is the same control wearing a different hat. A table with no maximum is a table with no worst case, and "no worst case" is how the August bill happened.

4

Alarm on the cause, not the total

ReadKeyRangeThroughputThrottleEvents and its write twin for the partition ceiling; the *AccountLimit* and *MaxOnDemandThroughput* pair for on-demand; WriteThrottleEvents for provisioned. ThrottledRequests is the summary, and it rounds four different incidents down to one.

Today's bill

What yours costs while the stack is down

The application stack is destroyed, so aws_dynamodb_table.users does not exist. One DynamoDB table in this account is still running: the Terraform state lock table in terraform/state-backend/, on-demand, holding at most one LockID item.

An apply takes the lock, writes the state digest and deletes the lock. Call it three write request units and a read:

3 write request units × $0.625 / 1,000,000 = $0.0000019 per apply 1 item of ~100 bytes × $0.25 / GB-month = $0.000000023 per month

Which is the good side of the metered model, and worth holding next to the complaint from Lesson 5.1 about rounding every charge in the vendor's direction. Both are true. A table nobody uses costs nothing, a table with the right key schema costs what the work costs, and a table with the wrong one costs what the whole table costs — every time somebody loads a page.

Check yourself

Four questions

A table takes a steady 500 writes a second, flat, around the clock — a sensor feed with no diurnal shape at all. Which mode, and what is the choice worth?

Provisioned, by a lot. Flat traffic is the one shape that can run near 100% utilisation, because there is no peak to leave headroom for.

On-demand: 500 × 3,600 × 730 = 1,314,000,000 writes a month = $821.25. Provisioned 500 WCU: 500 × $0.00065 × 730 = $237.25. The saving is $584 a month, or 71% — which is 1 − 1/3.46, the most the ratio can ever give you. A one-year reserved purchase takes it further still.

Notice what makes this case easy: no variance means no forecasting. The spikier the traffic, the worse provisioned looks and the more work it takes to prove which side of 28.9% you are on.

An on-demand table with no maximum configured. A deploy ships a bug that retries PutItem in a tight loop, and it runs for twenty minutes before anyone notices. What is the exposure, and what would have stopped it?

The ceiling is the account's table-level quota, 40,000 write request units a second: 40,000 × 1,200 s = 48,000,000 writes = $30. If the table was cold, warm throughput holds the first stretch to 4,000 writes/s and it is nearer $3. On a table that has already peaked higher, the full $30 — and $90 an hour for as long as it runs.

What stops it: on_demand_throughput { max_write_request_units = … }, plus an alarm on WriteMaxOnDemandThroughputThrottleEvents so the throttling is visible rather than just slow.

What does not stop it: IAM, because there is no permission to spend; the SDK, which retries throttles on your behalf; and a budget alarm, which tells you hours later. And one uncomfortable observation — switching to provisioned would also have stopped it, because throttling is the default state of a provisioned table under a runaway. That is the single safety property provisioned has for free.

You shard signup_month into ten keys to get past 1,000 writes a second. What did you make more expensive, and what did you not fix?

More expensive: reads. One month is now ten partition keys, so "users who signed up in September" becomes ten Query calls instead of one — ten round trips, each with its own capacity rounding. Expensive a second way too: the shard count is now part of the contract with every reader, and changing it means rewriting the key of every item.

Not fixed: the base table, which was never the problem — hash(email) already spreads perfectly. Nor the monotonic created_at sort key inside each shard. Nor anything about reads of the index, which were never concentrated; the index was only ever write-hot.

The general shape: sharding converts a throughput limit into a fan-out cost, and you can only do it cheaply before the data exists.

A provisioned table, auto scaling on, min 100 / max 4,000 WCU, target 70%. At 09:00 a marketing email goes to 200,000 users and the writes arrive over about two minutes. Walk through 09:00 to 09:06.

About 1,700 writes a second arriving at a table provisioned near 100–150 WCU.

First, burst capacity: up to 300 seconds of unused capacity at 100 WCU is roughly 30,000 writes, spent in the first twenty seconds. Then throttling, as ProvisionedThroughputExceededException — which the SDK retries, so the application sees latency and a growing queue rather than errors.

Auto scaling needs two consecutive minutes of breach to trigger, then the CloudWatch alarm may add a few minutes, then UpdateTable takes several more, and the docs say plainly that requests above the old capacity are throttled throughout. So expect four to eight minutes of degradation, finishing at about the time the campaign does. The capacity graph afterwards shows 4,000 WCU provisioned for a spike that is already over, and 15 consecutive data points before it comes down.

The fix is not a higher target or a lower minimum. It is knowing the spike is coming: scheduled scaling before 09:00, or on-demand, where a table that has previously peaked at 1,700 absorbs 1,700 instantly. Auto scaling is for shapes that repeat slowly. A known event is a schedule, and an unknown event is on-demand.
