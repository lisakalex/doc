Say Again?

The English an interviewer actually uses — idioms, hedges and engineering slang, in plain words. None of it is technical, all of it will be said to you, and none of it is in the abbreviations sheet.

In the interview

What they say, and what they want

walk me throughexplain it step by step, in order — they want the sequence and your reasoning at each step, not a summary. Start at the beginning and do not skip.
at a high levelthe short version, no detail — two or three sentences. If you start naming config options you have misread the question.
deep dive / drill down / dig intogo deeper on one specific part — they are interested, not unhappy. Stay on the one thing they named.
off the top of your headguess now, from memory, no checking — they accept that it may be wrong. Refusing to answer is worse than an approximate answer.
ballpark / back of the envelopea rough number, right order of size — "a few hundred dollars a month" is a good answer. "I would need to calculate it" is not.
sanity checka quick test that something is not obviously wrong — not a full test. "Let me sanity check that" = "let me confirm the basics".
red flaga warning sign; something that would worry them — "what would be a red flag in that code review?" = what would make you refuse to approve it.
low-hanging fruitthe easy wins you would do first — cheap changes with obvious benefit. From fruit you can pick without a ladder.
trade-offwhat you lose in exchange for what you gain — the most common interview question shape. Every answer should name both sides.
talk me through your thinkingsay the reasoning out loud, including the wrong turns — they are grading the process, not only the answer.
what would you do differentlycriticise your own past work — a test of honesty. "Nothing" is the wrong answer.
tell me about a time when…a behavioural question — give one real story — one specific incident with a beginning and an end. Not "usually I would…".
STARSituation, Task, Action, Result — the structure expected for the question above. Say what was happening, what you had to do, what you did, how it ended.
does that make sense?usually "was my explanation clear?" — it is rarely a test of you. "Yes" or "not quite — can you repeat the part about X" are both fine.
let's park that / take it offlinestop discussing this now, maybe later — not a criticism. Move on without arguing.
any questions for us?a scored part of the interview, not politeness — have two ready. Ask about on-call, deploy frequency, or how decisions get made.
nice to have vs must haveoptional versus required — useful in your own answers: "versioning is a must-have on state, a nice-to-have on avatars".
in angerin real production use, under real load — British. "I have not used Kubernetes in anger" = only in tutorials, never for real. A very useful honest phrase.

Saying you do not know

How to be honest without sounding weak

"it depends"correct only if you immediately say what it depends on — alone it sounds evasive. "It depends on whether the bucket is overwritten or append-only" is a strong answer.
"my instinct is X, but I would verify"commit to an answer and name how you would check it — this is what senior sounds like. Guessing with no check sounds careless; refusing to guess sounds timid.
"I have not hit that in practice"honest admission of a gap, without apology — follow with what you would read or test first.
"I would push back on that"I disagree, and I would say so at work — interviewers often want to see this. Disagreeing politely is a skill they are testing.
"that is a fair point"you are right and I am changing my answer — safe to use. Changing your mind on evidence scores well.
hand-wave / hand-wavyvague, skipping the hard part — if someone says your answer is hand-wavy, they want the specific mechanism, not more description.
straw mana rough first proposal, offered to be attacked — "here is a straw man" = "this is not my final answer, criticise it". A genuinely useful thing to offer.
devil's advocatearguing the opposite on purpose, not sincerely — "let me play devil's advocate" means the next thing they say is a test, not their real opinion.

Engineering slang

Words used as if everyone knows them

blast radius taughthow much breaks if this one thing fails — from explosives. "Separate accounts limit the blast radius" = a mistake in one cannot reach the other.
happy paththe run where nothing goes wrong — "it works on the happy path" is a criticism: the error cases were not handled.
edge case / corner casea rare input or state that breaks the normal logic — empty list, zero, a name with an apostrophe, a leap second.
foot-guna feature that makes it easy to hurt yourself — from "shoot yourself in the foot". force_destroy = true is a foot-gun.
tech debtshortcuts taken earlier that cost you time now — like a loan: you borrowed speed and you are paying interest.
greenfield / brownfieldbuilding new from nothing / working inside an existing system — from construction: empty land versus a site with old buildings on it.
yak shavinga long chain of unrelated tasks before the real one — you wanted to fix a bug, so you upgraded a library, which needed a new runtime, which needed… none of it was the bug.
bikesheddingarguing at length about something trivial — a committee approves a nuclear plant quickly and spends hours on the colour of the bike shed, because everyone has an opinion about sheds.
rubber duckingexplaining a problem out loud to find your own mistake — traditionally to a toy duck. Explaining forces the gap into the open.
cattle, not petsservers should be replaceable, not repaired by hand — you name a pet and nurse it when sick; you number cattle and replace them.
snowflakea server or environment configured by hand, unique, impossible to recreate — "no two snowflakes are alike". The thing Infrastructure as Code exists to prevent.
drift taughtreality no longer matches the declared config — someone changed it in the console. terraform plan is how you detect it.
bus factorhow many people must disappear before the project stops — "bus factor of one" = only one person understands it. A risk, not a compliment.
toilmanual repetitive work that grows with scale and adds no lasting value — a precise SRE term, not just "boring work". The thing you are meant to automate away.
shift leftcatch problems earlier in the process — from a left-to-right diagram of build → test → deploy. Security scanning in CI instead of before release.
paved road / golden paththe supported, easy way to do something — you may leave it, but then you support yourself.
dogfoodingusing your own product internally — from "eat your own dog food".
noisy neighbouranother tenant on shared hardware consuming what you needed — your performance drops and nothing you did caused it.
thundering herdeverything retries at the same moment and overwhelms the thing recovering — fixed with jitter: random delay so retries spread out.
cold startthe slow first request, before anything is warm — Lambda spinning up, a cache that is empty, a JIT that has not compiled yet.
back pressurea system telling its caller to slow down instead of collapsing — queue full, request rejected. Healthier than accepting work you cannot do.
graceful degradation taughtlosing a feature instead of the whole service — avatars fail to load but login still works.
flaky testa test that passes and fails without the code changing — worse than a failing test, because people learn to ignore it.
smoke testa tiny check that the thing basically works after a change — from electronics: power it on and see whether smoke comes out.
canaryrelease to a small share of traffic first and watch — from caged birds taken into coal mines to detect gas before it reached the miners.
blue / greentwo full environments; switch traffic between them — rollback is switching back, not redeploying.
big bangchanging everything at once — always said as a warning.
single pane of glassone screen showing everything — usually something a vendor is selling you.

Incidents and on-call

The vocabulary of things going wrong

on callthe person responsible for responding if it breaks, right now — usually a weekly rota. "Who is on call?" = who gets woken up.
paged / got pagedautomatically alerted, often at night — from the old pager device. "It paged at 3am" = an alert fired and woke someone.
SEV1 / P1the most serious incident level — numbers go down as severity goes up. SEV1 usually means customer-visible and total.
incident commanderthe person coordinating the response, not fixing it — they decide and communicate; others type. A deliberate separation.
war roomthe call or room where an incident is handled — increasingly avoided as a term; "incident channel" is the calmer modern word.
mitigate vs fixstop the bleeding now vs remove the cause later — a restart mitigates; the code change fixes. In an incident you mitigate first, always.
rollback / roll forwardreturn to the previous version / ship a new version that fixes it — the choice is usually about which is faster and safer, not which is tidier.
post-mortemthe written review after an incident — Latin for "after death". A blameless post-mortem deliberately excludes who to blame, so people tell the truth.
RCARoot Cause Analysis — finding the underlying cause. Modern SRE is sceptical that there is exactly one.
runbook / playbookstep-by-step instructions for a known situation — written so a tired person at 3am can follow it without thinking.
escalatehand it to someone more senior or more specialised — not an admission of failure. Escalating early is usually correct.
change freezeno deploys during a risky period — Black Friday, year end, during an incident.
game day / fire drilla practice incident, deliberately caused — to test whether the runbooks and the people actually work.
chaos engineeringbreaking production on purpose, in controlled ways — the discipline that grew out of game days. Netflix's Chaos Monkey is the origin.
firefightingconstantly reacting to problems, never preventing them — said with frustration. "We are firefighting" = nothing planned is getting done.
KTLOKeep The Lights On — the work required just to stay running. Budget discussions separate KTLO from new work.

Team and process

Meeting words

stand-upa short daily status meeting — historically held standing so it stays short. What you did, what you will do, what is blocking you.
blocked / blockercannot continue until something else happens — saying you are blocked early is expected, not a complaint.
retroretrospective — a team meeting about how the work went — about process, not about an incident.
sprint / backlog / refinementa fixed work period / the list of not-yet-done work / the meeting where that list is clarified — "grooming" is the older word for refinement.
RFC / design doc / ADRa written proposal circulated before building — ADR = Architecture Decision Record: what was decided, and why, kept in the repo.
LGTMLooks Good To Me — code review approval.
nit / nitpicka small comment that should not block approval — "nit: typo in the comment". Prefixing with "nit:" is a courtesy that says "fix if you like, I am not blocking you".
WIPWork In Progress — on a pull request it means "do not review or merge yet".
pingsend a short message to get attention — "ping me when it is done". From the network tool.
loop inadd someone to the conversation — "looping in the security team".
circle back / touch basetalk again later — often means "not now". If nobody sets a time, it may mean never.
buy-inagreement and support from the people who matter — "we need buy-in from the platform team" = they must agree before this can work.
stakeholderanyone affected by the decision — not only managers.
EOD / COB / EOWEnd Of Day / Close Of Business / End Of Week — a deadline. Check which time zone.
OOOOut Of Office — away, not working.
heads-upadvance warning — "a heads-up: I am deploying at 4". Informational, not a request.

Disagreement in disguise

Phrases that mean the opposite of how they sound

"interesting…"often "I disagree" or "I do not believe that" — tone decides. If it is followed by a pause, expect a challenge.
"I am not sure I follow"usually "I think you are wrong", said politely — British especially. Treat it as a request to justify, not to repeat.
"with respect…"disagreement is coming immediately — the phrase warns you; it does not soften much.
"have you considered…""you have missed something" — a gentle way to point out a gap. Take it seriously.
"that is one way to do it""I would not do it that way" — ask what they would do instead.
"let us agree to disagree"the discussion is over without agreement — usually ends the topic. If the decision still matters, escalate instead.
"the elephant in the room"the obvious problem nobody is mentioning — naming it is usually welcome.
"boil the ocean"attempt something impossibly large — "let us not boil the ocean" = reduce the scope.
"kick the can down the road"delay a decision that will only get harder — always a criticism.
"moving the goalposts"changing the requirements after work started — a complaint about unfairness.
"a hill to die on"an argument worth losing your position over — "that is not a hill to die on" = concede this one.
"in the weeds"lost in detail, having missed the larger point — "we are in the weeds" = step back up.
"scope creep"the work quietly growing beyond what was agreed — each addition is small; the total is not.
"closed book"from memory, without looking anything up — from exams. "Open book" means you may use your notes. The phrase that sent you here.
