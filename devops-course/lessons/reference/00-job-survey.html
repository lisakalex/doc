Nine in Ten, Checked

I scanned 5,393 job postings, kept the 147 with a genuine DevOps-family job title, and counted. Your three are the top three — Kubernetes, AWS and Terraform, in that order. But all three appear together in 12% of postings, not ninety. Here is the data, the method, what I got wrong on the first attempt, and the five changes it makes to the curriculum.

My first attempt was wrong, and here is how I knew

Run one: 252 postings, Docker at 6%, only 30% naming any cloud, and "Nomad" at 78% of RemoteOK — which is a remote-jobs board, so it was matching digital nomad. Three impossible numbers meant the corpus was contaminated: my filter had admitted general software postings that merely mentioned DevOps among several roles, and I was counting the whole posting's stack. I threw it away and rebuilt with title-only matching. The lesson is the one from Where the Mock Lies: an implausible result is evidence about your instrument.

The skew you must hold in mind. 77% of the sample is Hacker News, so this measures US startups and scale-ups posting their own roles. It will understate enterprise tooling — Azure, Jenkins, ServiceNow, VMware, Ansible — and overstate modern tooling. It also measures about 1% of the market, and the wrong 1% for a UK candidate: see the correction below, which supersedes the explanation offered in the cards above.

Why your number was higher

Not selection bias, as I first assumed. Reed UK is 77% recruitment-agency postings; my sample is 0%. Agency adverts list every technology the client mentioned. See the correction below — your 90% is probably right for your market.

There is a second, more innocent reason: posting length. The median posting here is 1,132 characters, because Hacker News adverts are three lines. A LinkedIn job description runs four to six thousand characters and lists everything anyone in the team has touched. Mine undercounts by brevity rather than by absence — so read my percentages as a floor, and the ranking as the reliable part.

2 · Grafana and Prometheus beat the managed tools, and CloudWatch is absent

Grafana 20%, Prometheus 16%, Datadog 10%, Elastic 5%, OpenTelemetry 1%. CloudWatch: zero postings. Splunk and New Relic: zero. So replacing CloudWatch with Prometheus and Grafana as the observability subject was the right call, and Grafana being the single most-named observability tool means the dashboard you build matters more than the storage engine behind it.

3 · Employers want CI/CD the idea, not CI/CD the product

"CI/CD" as a concept appears in 29% of postings. The tools barely register: GitHub Actions 7%, GitLab CI 4%, Jenkins 1%, CircleCI and Buildkite under 1%. Which means pipeline design is the examinable skill and tool trivia is not — exactly how Module 26 is built. It also means the Jenkins experience you do not have is not worth acquiring.

4 · Python is the DevOps language, and it is already yours

Python 33%, Go 17%, Bash 8%. Python is named as often as AWS. You are a Python developer, which is a genuine advantage over candidates who arrived through system administration — and the weekly scripting track should lean into it rather than treating it as revision. Go at 17% is worth reading fluently, because half the tools you will operate are written in it, but not worth learning to write well.

5 · EKS is almost never named, and the module stays anyway

"EKS" appears explicitly in 5% of postings. But AWS and Kubernetes appear together in 19%, and there is no such thing as running Kubernetes on AWS without running EKS or building something worse. The advert says "AWS" and "Kubernetes" in separate bullet points; the job is EKS. The VPC CNI exhausting your subnet does not care whether the advert mentioned it.

So twelve months of the open web produced 147 DevOps postings, while one UK board has 630 standing at this instant and LinkedIn reports seven thousand in the UK alone. The open APIs cover something on the order of 1% of the addressable market, and they cover a structurally different slice of it.

The structural difference, which is the real finding

Reed's own facets: 484 of those 630 postings are placed by recruitment agencies and 146 by direct employers — 77% agency. Hacker News is 0% agency by construction; it is founders and engineers posting their own roles in three lines.

Agency adverts are keyword-dense, because the recruiter lists every technology the client mentioned plus everything that might match a candidate search. Direct-employer adverts say "we run Go on GCP" and stop. That, not filtered searching, is why you saw AWS, Terraform and Kubernetes in nine adverts out of ten and I measured twelve per cent. We were counting different populations, and if you are applying in the UK, yours is the one that matters.

What survives from my survey is the relative ordering within a population. What does not survive is any absolute claim about prevalence. I also wrote here that the near-zeros were safe — that CloudWatch at zero and Terraform beating CloudFormation 29:1 were gaps too wide to be artefacts. The next section measures 662 UK adverts and finds CloudWatch at 11.3% and the ratio at 4.9:1. They were artefacts. Only the direction held.

What this changes about how you prepare

Nothing about the learning — depth still beats breadth, and no keyword gets you through a debugging question. But it changes the CV. In a market where three quarters of adverts are agency-placed and screened on term matching, a CV that demonstrates depth in three technologies and never mentions the other twelve will be filtered out before an engineer sees it. Learn depth-first; write the CV so the keywords are present and truthful.

Second survey, 5 October 2026 — this one supersedes the figures above

The real UK market, measured: 662 adverts

An Adzuna API key arrived, so the survey no longer depends on three key-free sources covering 1% of the market. This run measures every advert on a national UK aggregator whose title contains “devops” — 662 of them, against 609,377 UK jobs in total. Where this section and the one above disagree, this one is right: it is a national market rather than a convenience sample, and the matching runs against complete advert text.

The method, and the two API behaviours that nearly ruined it

Adzuna truncates the description it returns to 500 characters — the preamble of a corporate advert, not its technology list. So this run counts no text. It asks the API how many adverts match and divides: title_only=devops gives the denominator, adding what_phrase=terraform gives the numerator. Matching happens server-side against the full advert, so truncation cannot suppress it, and the whole survey costs 81 API calls.

Two behaviours had to be measured rather than assumed, and both bite:

1. title_only ANDs its keywords. title_only="devops sre" returns 3 adverts — the ones whose title contains both words — not the union. Role families must be surveyed one term at a time.

2. what_or does not OR the phrases you give it. It splits them into single words and ORs those. what_or="istio,linkerd,service mesh" returns 446, which is exactly what what_or="service" returns. A first run built on what_or reported GCP at 85% and service mesh at 67% of all adverts; both were the count for a common English word. That run was discarded. What this changes, beyond the numbers

Jenkins is not a paragraph. I recommended one paragraph on the evidence of a single posting in 147. It is named in 29.5% of UK DevOps adverts — the most-named CI tool, ahead of GitHub Actions and GitLab. That recommendation was wrong and is withdrawn.

PowerShell exists. It never appeared in my first survey. A quarter of UK DevOps adverts name it, which is the visible edge of a Windows-shop market that US startup boards do not show at all.

The near-zeros did not all survive. I wrote that CloudWatch at zero was too wide a gap to be a sampling artefact. It was a sampling artefact: 11.3% here. The Terraform-to-CloudFormation ratio held its direction but not its size — 4.9:1, not 29:1. Relative ordering survived; the magnitudes did not, including the ones I said would.

Azure is not a sidebar. At 45.2% it is named in nearly half of UK DevOps adverts. Four hours of Azure literacy was sized against a 38% figure from a small EU subset and happens to survive this, but it is the floor, not the answer.

The core does not move. AWS, Kubernetes, Terraform, Python and Linux land within ten per cent of each other in both populations. Whatever market you end up in, that is the stack, and that is the strongest evidence this course has produced for the shape of the curriculum.

Everything that moves, moves along one axis. Cloud-native and observability tooling skews to direct employers — OpenTofu ×11, OpenTelemetry ×4.3, Pulumi ×3, Datadog ×1.7, the word “observability” itself ×1.6. Enterprise and legacy tooling skews to the aggregator — Jenkins ×0.2, CloudWatch ×0.1, Splunk ×0.1, Ansible ×0.5. These are not two samples of one market. They are two markets.

What that means for the plan

The Jenkins question has an answer now. I withdrew the “one paragraph” recommendation an hour ago on the aggregator's 29.5%. Direct employers name it in 4.9%. Both are true: Jenkins is how you get through an agency screen for an enterprise role, and nearly absent at product companies. Learn enough to discuss it; do not spend a module on it.

SRE practice vocabulary is almost invisible on the aggregator and everywhere at product companies. On-call appears in 40.1% of direct-employer adverts and SLOs in 25.9%. Those are practices rather than tools, they cannot be picked up from documentation, and they are what the interview will actually probe.

Go is not optional at this end of the market. 54.3%, second only to Python. I previously called it “worth reading fluently, not worth writing well” on a 17% figure from the Hacker News corpus. At product companies it is half the adverts, and the reading-fluency recommendation is the floor rather than the target.

The uncomfortable finding

Twenty-nine per cent of adverts name no technology at all

Forty-two of the 147 postings mention no cloud, no orchestrator, no language and no tool. They ask for "a strong infrastructure background", "experience owning production systems", "someone who has scaled a platform". Those postings cannot be matched by keyword, and they are nearly a third of the market.

Which tells you something the technology table cannot. For that third, the filter is not your skill list — it is whether your CV and your project read like someone who has operated something. That is what the project track and the three postmortems in Module 31 are for, and it is the half of the preparation that keyword-counting makes invisible.

And the seniority note

Across the sample, "senior" appears far more often than "mid" or "junior" — which is partly real and partly that almost nobody advertises a role as mid-level; they advertise "DevOps Engineer" and decide the level in the interview. Do not filter yourself out of postings that say senior. Apply, and let them calibrate you.

Reproduce it

The survey is a script, so re-run it in three months

The corpus is a moving target — Hacker News adds a thread every month, and the whole point of this exercise is that a claim about the job market decays. Re-running it in February tells you whether Kubernetes is still at 41% and whether OpenTofu has moved off 1%. The script is in the repo so that the answer in three months is a measurement rather than a memory.
