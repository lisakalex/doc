Curriculum v4 · re-cut against a placement result

The Plan, Re-Cut

Twenty-four weeks rebuilt around what one person actually knows, rather than what a market survey says a role requires. The scope did not shrink. The order changed completely, and two phases got shorter because the experience was already there under a different name.

216 hours 24 weeks at 9 h 7 phases project runs from week 3

Why it changed

The assessment found the opposite of what I had assumed

The previous plan assumed a developer who knew Linux, Docker and networking and needed AWS. A twenty-question adaptive placement found the reverse on both counts, and the correction runs in two directions at once.

What was worse than assumed

Linux fundamentals, AWS mechanisms, Terraform and Kubernetes all scored at or near zero. Not the vocabulary — that was present throughout — but every mechanism underneath it. The signature of learning from documentation rather than from operating things, which matters because more reading is what produced it.

What was better than assumed

Two years of production responsibility with deploy and rollback authority. Jenkins pipelines maintained in anger. Ansible on real servers. Python running unattended in CI and depended on by other engineers. Build-once-deploy-many understood so thoroughly it was the only full-marks answer in the whole assessment.

The one that buys the most time

Ansible is declarative, idempotent and safe to re-run — the same model as Terraform, applied to a machine's configuration rather than to whether the machine exists. Two years of using that model without anyone naming it means Terraform needs its mechanisms explained, not its philosophy. Twelve hours becomes nine, and the phase starts at state rather than at syntax.

Four rules the re-cut follows

How the order was decided

Rule 1

Mechanism before vocabulary. Every gap found was a named thing with nothing underneath it. No lesson stops at what something is called.

Rule 2

Build on the assets, do not re-teach them. CI/CD becomes a translation exercise. Terraform arrives through Ansible. Containers use the Java and Maven stack already known.

Rule 3

Containers are the critical path. Zero in production, 44.6% of adverts alone, and the prerequisite for the 62% that want Kubernetes. They move from phase 3 to phase 2, directly behind the Linux they depend on.

Rule 4

The project manufactures the experience the job cannot. Never on-call, never more than one application and a database. Both are fixed by building something and then deliberately breaking it.

The seven phases

Twenty-four weeks, in order

0

Name what you already do weeks 1–2 14 h

You have been doing CI/CD for two years without the vocabulary for it. This phase converts tacit experience into things you can say in an interview, and repairs the mechanisms under the tools you already run.

  • Artifact promotion: why build-once-deploy-many is the rule you already follow, and what breaks without it
  • Jenkins → GitHub Actions and GitLab CI: the same concepts, different syntax. You translate your own deploy pipeline as the exercise
  • Secrets in a pipeline: Jenkins credentials, Parameter Store, Secrets Manager — what each one actually guarantees, and OIDC federation so the pipeline holds no key at all
  • Ansible idempotency, named: changed_when, creates, and why shell and command break the model
  • Python that survives leaving your laptop: virtualenv, pinned requirements, why it failed on the server

Two weeks, and your CV changes in week two.

1

The machine underneath weeks 3–6 36 h

Your lowest score, and it sits beneath everything else in the plan. Not an introduction — this is the layer below the commands you already run every day.

  • Processes and file descriptors: why df and du disagree, lsof +L1, log rotation done wrong
  • Memory: RSS against page cache, why free looks alarming and is fine, the OOM killer and how cgroups kill differently
  • systemd: units, timers instead of cron, journald — and why your 2am cron job failed silently
  • Networking: interfaces, 0.0.0.0 against loopback, ss, DNS resolution and the five-second timeout, TLS handshakes
  • Users, groups and permissions from below — the things your Ansible playbooks have been setting for two years

Every lesson ends by breaking it on purpose and fixing it.

2

Containers, properly weeks 7–10 36 h

You have the object model — image, container, volume — and none of the build or network side. This is the critical path: it is 44.6% of adverts on its own and it is the prerequisite for the 62% that want Kubernetes.

  • Layers and copy-on-write: why ten containers from one image do not use ten images of disk
  • The build cache and instruction order, on your actual Java/Maven stack. Multi-stage builds, 700 MB down to 200 MB
  • Container networking: bridge, published ports, DNS between containers, and why your service bound to localhost is unreachable
  • Volumes: named, bind and anonymous — including the Postgres trap that strands your data in a volume with a hash for a name
  • Registries and image provenance, which is JFrog again at a different layer
  • Compose for several services locally — your first system with more than one moving part

Exercise: containerise a Java service of your own, end to end.

3

AWS, mechanism first weeks 11–14 36 h

You scored 2/8 while having shipped through CodeCommit and read secrets from Parameter Store. The exposure is real; the mechanisms are missing. This phase is the mechanisms.

  • IAM: principals, how a request is evaluated, explicit deny beating every allow, SCPs and permissions boundaries
  • Roles against users, instance profiles, and IMDS — where credentials actually come from, IMDSv2, and why SSRF cost Capital One 100 million records
  • VPC: route tables are the only thing that makes a subnet public, IGW against NAT, gateway endpoints, security groups being stateful against NACLs
  • S3 and storage mechanics; KMS and envelope encryption, under the Parameter Store you already use
  • Cost as an engineering concern: NAT gateway per-gigabyte, data transfer, the invoice that surprises people

Runs on the free moto sandbox first, real AWS only where the sandbox lies.

4

Terraform, through Ansible weeks 15–17 27 h

Nine hours shorter than the original plan, because you already own the hard half. Declarative, idempotent, converge-on-rerun — that is Ansible, and you have used it for two years. We skip the philosophy and start at state.

  • What is in state and why the cloud cannot be read instead of it
  • Remote backends and locking: what two simultaneous applies do to an unlocked state file
  • What a half-failed apply leaves behind — there is no rollback, and believing there is will hurt you in an incident
  • Drift, import, moved blocks, lifecycle, and bringing hand-built resources under management without downtime
  • Modules, variables, outputs, and where Terraform stops and Ansible starts

Exercise: rebuild everything you clicked together in phase 3, as code.

5

Kubernetes weeks 18–21 36 h

Zero, from a standing start, and named in 62.1% of UK adverts. The longest road on the list and the one with the largest market premium. It gets full weight and no trimming.

  • Pods, deployments, services, ingress — and what the control loop is actually doing
  • The debugging loop: CrashLoopBackOff, describe before logs, --previous, exit codes 0, 1 and 137
  • Requests and limits, QoS classes, OOMKilled against CPU throttled — the asymmetry that explains every argument about limits
  • Probes, rolling updates, and the thirty-second window where readiness passes and users get 502s
  • EKS specifics: IRSA and Pod Identity, the ALB controller, node groups against Fargate, EBS CSI and availability-zone affinity

Your phase 2 containers, on kind first, then on EKS from phase 4's Terraform.

6

Operating it, and the on-call you never had weeks 22–24 27 h

Zero score, 61% of direct-employer adverts, and the only honest substitute for a pager. Half teaching, half drills.

  • Metrics, logs and traces: which answers which question, and why going straight to logs costs you the night
  • Percentiles rather than averages — p50 flat and p99 tripled is a different problem from everything being slower
  • Prometheus and Grafana against your own running system
  • SLOs, SLIs and error budgets: 99.9% is 43 minutes a month, and the budget exists to end an argument
  • Alerting that is actionable, and the dead man's switch — alerting on the absence of success, which is what your 2am cron job needed
  • Six incident drills. I break your system at a layer you have learned. You diagnose it and write the postmortem.

You finish with six written incidents about a system you own.

The spine

One project, from week 3 to week 24

Not a phase. It runs alongside every phase from week three and absorbs roughly a third of the total hours, because the diagnosis was that reading produces vocabulary and only operating produces mechanisms.

What it has to be, and why each constraint is there

More than one service. Everything operated so far has been one application and its database. Partial failure — one thing down while another is up, retries, timeouts, a queue filling — only exists once services talk to each other, and it is most of what makes this job hard.

Terraform provisions it, CI deploys to it, Prometheus watches it. That three-way join is the job description. Being able to say it about something you built, and then answer questions about the parts that went wrong, is the thing a certificate cannot provide.

It gets broken on purpose, six to eight times. Each break at a layer just learned: a full disk held open by a deleted file, an OOMKilled pod, an unlocked state file applied twice, a readiness probe that passes while users get 502s. You diagnose it and write the postmortem.

Why that last one matters more than it sounds: interviews ask you to walk through an incident you handled, and “I have been in the room” is a visibly weaker answer than “I was paged, here is what I checked first and what I got wrong.” Six written incidents about your own system is the closest honest substitute for a pager you have never carried.

The honest part

Whether twenty-four weeks is enough

For the market this experience fits, yes, and it was not going to be before the second half of the assessment. Jenkins at 29.5% and Ansible at 31.9% of UK adverts are the enterprise and agency half of the market — the larger half, where 77% of postings are agency-placed. That CV already half-fits. What it is missing is AWS, Terraform and containers, which is exactly what the twenty-four weeks adds.

For product companies, no. Go at 54%, GCP at 52%, Kubernetes-native operations and SRE practice as a working vocabulary — that is a different population and a longer road. It is a reasonable target for the year after this one, not for month six.

Where the plan breaks if it breaks

Phases 2 and 5 are 72 hours between them and they are one continuous dependency: containers, then Kubernetes on top of containers. If the schedule slips, it will slip there, and the temptation will be to compress Kubernetes to catch up.

Do the opposite. Cut the depth of phase 6 and keep the drills, or extend to twenty-eight weeks. A shallow pass at the 62% of the market that wants Kubernetes is worth very little; adverts ask what broke and why, not what the objects are called.
