Three Things, Properly

Nine listings in ten name AWS, Terraform and Kubernetes. So the plan now spends two thirds of its hours on exactly those three — and fixes the gap that market data exposed in the last version, which taught Kubernetes on a laptop and AWS beside it and never taught the thing the listings actually describe: EKS.

Why Linux and networking survive the cut

They are not competing with Kubernetes; they are what Kubernetes debugging is. You cannot diagnose CrashLoopBackOff without exit codes and signals, or a Service returning 503 without DNS and TCP, or OOMKilled without cgroups. Fourteen hours here is what makes the seventy-two hours after it land. What I did cut is the breadth that was there for its own sake — page-cache internals, MTU trivia, systemd beyond units.

One caution about optimising from listings

Listings are written by recruiters from templates and name technologies. Interviews are run by engineers and test debugging and judgement. Both signals are real, and they point at different things: the listing gets you in the room, the drills get you the offer. So I have leaned the coverage toward your three and kept every timed drill. If something has to give later, give up a reading module, never a drill.

The new module

Module 23 · EKS, which is not Kubernetes

Everything in Modules 18 to 22 runs on kind and teaches Kubernetes. None of it teaches the parts that only exist when AWS runs your control plane, and those are precisely what an interviewer for an AWS-plus-Kubernetes role will ask about.

TopicWhy it only exists on EKS
Control plane vs data planeAWS runs the API server, etcd and scheduler; you run the nodes. The split decides what you can debug and what you open a support case about.
The AWS VPC CNI and IP exhaustionEvery pod gets a real VPC IP address from your subnet. A /24 subnet holds 251 pods, not 251 nodes — the classic EKS outage, and pure Kubernetes has no equivalent.
Managed node groups vs Fargate vs KarpenterThree capacity models with different cost shapes, scaling latencies and constraints. "Which would you pick and why" is a standard question.
IRSA and EKS Pod IdentityHow a pod gets AWS credentials without static keys — the direct continuation of Module 2's OIDC federation, and the specimen repo already does it.
AWS Load Balancer ControllerAn Ingress becomes a real ALB, with target groups, subnet tags and annotations. Ingress on kind teaches none of this.
EBS CSI driver and add-onsStorage is an AWS volume with AZ affinity, so a pod can become unschedulable for a reason that does not exist on a laptop.
Version upgrades and extended supportControl plane then nodes then add-ons, in that order. Miss the window and the price goes from $0.10 to $0.60 per cluster-hour — a 6× bill for standing still.
Provisioning all of it in TerraformThe join. Cluster, node groups, OIDC provider, IRSA roles, add-ons and controllers as code — which is the thing the job adverts are actually describing.

This is the one module that costs real money

EKS has no free tier: $0.10 per cluster-hour, charged whether anything runs on it or not. Two t3.medium nodes for a three-hour session comes to roughly $0.70 all in, so six sessions across the course is about $5 — provided you terraform destroy every single time. You have already paid the tuition for forgetting: $34.96 in August 2026, which at $0.10 an hour is 350 hours of an empty control plane.

The project

Build the sentence the listings are describing

The project track starts in week 3 and its spine is deliberately the three-way join, because that join is the job description: Terraform provisions EKS, CI deploys to it, Prometheus watches it. If you can say that about something you built, and then answer twenty minutes of questions about why each piece is the way it is, you are interviewing as a mid-level DevOps engineer rather than as a developer who has read about it.

1

Weeks 3–6 · the service and its infrastructure

Something small and genuinely yours — a URL shortener, a webhook relay, a feed poller. Container it properly, then Terraform the VPC, a bucket, a table and the IAM it needs. By week 6 it runs somewhere real.

2

Weeks 7–10 · the Terraform becomes defensible

Refactor it into modules with a remote backend, three environments from one module, variable validation, and terraform test in CI. This is the part most candidates have never done and every Terraform question is about.

3

Weeks 11–18 · onto Kubernetes, then onto EKS

Helm chart, probes, resources, a NetworkPolicy, an HPA. Run it on kind all through the phase, and in week 18 stand the same chart up on a real EKS cluster your own Terraform created — with IRSA, an ALB Ingress and the add-ons. Then destroy it.

4

Weeks 19–24 · delivered and observed

GitHub Actions with OIDC, image signing, Argo CD syncing the chart, Prometheus metrics, one Grafana dashboard, an SLO, a runbook, and three postmortems from deliberately broken deployments. Then a README that defends every decision and names what you would change.

Checkpoints

Week 10: the project is deployed and its Terraform is modular and tested — if not, Phase 3 needs another week and Operate loses one. Week 18: it has run on real EKS at least once. Week 20: start applying, four weeks before the plan ends. Interviews find remaining gaps faster than any syllabus can.

Honesty

What two thirds on three technologies cannot buy

Weighting the plan toward what the listings name makes you legible to the filter that reads CVs. It does not change what happens in the room. The three things still missing at the end of this are production operational history, experience of scale, and a year of carrying something — and no curriculum supplies those. Module 31's simulated incidents and the real cost incident you lived through are what you will have. Say so plainly; candidates who name the edge of their experience interview better than candidates who paper over it.

And one risk worth naming in writing, because it is the predictable failure of this plan: AWS Modules 1–5 finish in week 6 and you may not interview until month six. The weekly interview-practice track exists for exactly that, rotating back over IAM evaluation, S3 lifecycle and KMS key policies so they are still live when somebody asks. Skip that track and the first third of this work evaporates.
